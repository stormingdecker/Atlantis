East Asian Theaters

Light-touch overview of campaign-relevant East Asian eras. Each era is sketched at the level of "what's happening here, who's operating, what mission shapes work."

For the deep era documents we have for the Mediterranean (Bronze Age, Prehistory, etc.), East Asia is currently a thinner secondary front. Detail will be promoted from this file to per-era files as specific campaigns require it.

The Strategic Picture

East Asia is EPOCH home turf. The Kunlun Shard's location in Tibet means:

  • EPOCH operates here at low spatial cost across all reachable eras (post-2300 BCE).
  • ATA pays significant spatial cost for any East Asian insertion.
  • EPOCH's embed network is dense in China, Japan, Korea, Mongolia, and Central Asia.
  • ATA's embed network in East Asia is sparse — each ATA embed there represents a major historical investment.

This makes East Asian campaigns structurally different from Mediterranean ops: ATA teams are the away team, paying more cost, with fewer local embeds, against an EPOCH-dense regional network. The mission shapes that work here are intelligence-gathering, targeted artifact recovery, anchor-establishment, and contesting specific EPOCH operations — rarely the multi-year shaping campaigns ATA runs in the Mediterranean.

See ../Core-Setting/Temporal-Gradient.md for the full anchor density model.

Imperial China

Pre-Imperial / Shang and Zhou (~1600–221 BCE)

  • Earliest accessible East Asian era for both agencies (post-2300 BCE for EPOCH; the deeper Bronze Age 4000–2300 BCE is ATA-exclusive but ATA has essentially no presence that far east, that far back — the spatial cost is prohibitive without anchor infrastructure that didn't exist yet).
  • EPOCH established its first East Asian embeds during the late Shang and early Zhou periods. Most are undercover (officials, scribes, military aides) rather than visible.
  • ATA presence is minimal. Any ATA op in this era pays full spatial cost from a Mediterranean-dense network.

Spring and Autumn / Warring States (~771–221 BCE)

  • EPOCH's regional consolidation period. Their operatives quietly shape philosophical schools (Confucianism, Legalism, Mohism), military doctrine, and statecraft toward outcomes favorable to eventual unification.
  • The Heshibi jade (see ../Artifacts/Candidates.md) emerges in this period — possible Atlantean cache surfacing from Bronze Age deposit.
  • Sun Tzu (~5th c. BCE) writes the Art of War. Probably mundane — a benchmark for what natural strategic genius looks like — but possibly an EPOCH-managed exposure of strategic doctrine that would propagate forward.

Qin Dynasty (221–206 BCE)

  • EPOCH op. Qin Shi Huang is EPOCH's most successful East Asian embed — see ../Embedded-Agents/Candidates.md.
  • The unification, standardization, and centralized administrative templates set East Asian institutional patterns for the next 2000 years.
  • Xu Fu's expedition (~219 BCE): unexplained disappearance of a fleet of 3000+ people. See Candidates.md.
  • The Mausoleum (sealed 210 BCE): unexcavated since. The single highest-value EPOCH artifact-recovery target in East Asia. See ../Artifacts/Candidates.md.

Han Dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE)

  • Post-Qin consolidation. EPOCH operates the dynasty's institutional development — Confucianism as state philosophy, the imperial examination system's precursor, the Han Code.
  • Silk Road opens under Emperor Wu (~140 BCE). This is structurally important: it physically connects Han China to Central Asia and eventually to the Mediterranean, creating a spatial bridge that lets both agencies extend operations along the route. EPOCH consolidates control over the eastern segment; ATA has minor presence on the western (Parthian/Roman) end.
  • Han falls (220 CE) into the Three Kingdoms period.

Three Kingdoms / Six Dynasties (220–589 CE)

  • Civil war and fragmentation. EPOCH largely allows the chaos because regional fragmentation doesn't threaten the unified-hegemony template downstream.
  • Zhuge Liang (181–234 CE) — possible ATA tech-leak operating from Shu. See Candidates.md.
  • Buddhism arrives from India and begins its East Asian spread. Religious-philosophical activity opens new operational vectors for both agencies.

Sui and Tang (581–907 CE)

  • The Tang period is a heavily contested middle-era zone in East Asia. ATA has built sufficient anchor infrastructure to operate here; EPOCH still has the home-field advantage.
  • Tang Taizong (r. 626–649) codifies the Tang Code — ATA codification op. See Candidates.md.
  • Wu Zetian (r. 690–705) — ATA reform op, female-emperor pattern. See Candidates.md.
  • Chang'an is the largest city in the world at this period and a major operational hub for both agencies.
  • The Silk Road is at peak activity. ATA-EPOCH cocktail-party tension on the trade routes (per ../Core-Setting/Orichalcum.md Agent-Sense doctrine).

Song through Yuan (960–1368 CE)

  • Song dynasty: commercial revolution, technological flourescence (gunpowder, movable type, magnetic compass). Mostly natural development with light agency touch.
  • Mongol conquests (~1200–1279 CE) — see ../Embedded-Agents/Candidates.md Genghis Khan entry. EPOCH-attributed (cautiously). Devastates the Islamic Golden Age in 1258 (sack of Baghdad), with major downstream consequences for the trajectory of Islamic intellectual development.
  • The Mongol invasions of Japan (1274, 1281) — the kamikaze typhoons. See Candidates.md.
  • Yuan dynasty (1271–1368): Mongol rule of China. Brief opening of cross-Eurasian contact (Marco Polo's era). Operationally important as an anchor-establishment window for both agencies extending into eastern Russia, the steppe, and China.

Ming and Qing (1368–1912 CE)

  • Zheng He's treasure fleets (1405–1433) — maritime expansion that then aborts. See Candidates.md.
  • Ming isolationism after 1433 closes the maritime door. EPOCH consolidates internal Chinese governance; ATA presence remains thin.
  • Qing dynasty (1644–1912) — Manchu conquest, then the long late-imperial period. ATA presence increases as European maritime contact gives ATA new anchor opportunities, but EPOCH has had centuries of head start.
  • The Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901) — anti-Western uprising. Reads possibly as EPOCH staging an early test of \"anti-Western backlash\" as a propaganda model, or as organic resistance both agencies tried to contain.

Imperial Japan

Asuka / Nara / Heian (538–1185 CE)

  • Shotoku Taishi (574–622 CE) — ATA codification + Buddhist establishment. See Candidates.md.
  • Heian period courtly culture. Murasaki Shikibu writes The Tale of Genji (~1010 CE) — possibly an ATA cultural-preservation operation establishing Japanese vernacular literary tradition as durable institutional memory.

Kamakura / Muromachi / Sengoku (1185–1600 CE)

  • Mongol invasions (1274, 1281) — see Imperial China above and Candidates.md.
  • Long period of internal political fragmentation followed by warring-states (Sengoku) consolidation. EPOCH dense regional presence; multiple operational threads.
  • Sekigahara (1600) — Tokugawa Ieyasu installation. See Candidates.md.

Edo / Tokugawa Shogunate (1603–1868 CE)

  • 250 years of internal stability and external isolation (sakoku). Faction attribution contested — see Candidates.md.
  • Period of artistic, literary, and intellectual flourescence within isolation. Many cultural-preservation operations both agencies value.

Meiji and Imperial Modernization (1868–1945 CE)

  • Meiji Restoration (1868) — Japan's rapid Westernization. Possibly ATA op (introducing democratic-modernizing influences into an EPOCH-dominant region). Possibly natural in response to gunboat diplomacy. Possibly both — ATA exploiting an organic crisis.
  • Late-Meiji and early Shōwa periods: militarization, imperial expansion, eventual catastrophe of WWII. Major operational period for both agencies.

Post-1945 Japan

  • Democratic consolidation under post-war Allied occupation — a major ATA success in EPOCH home turf. Modern Japan is one of ATA's most important East Asian institutional anchors.
  • EPOCH does not let this pass uncontested — internal Japanese politics throughout the late 20th century show evidence of EPOCH counter-influence operations.

Korea

Three Kingdoms and Goryeo (~57 BCE – 1392 CE)

  • Cultural and institutional transmission from China dominates the period. EPOCH operates indirectly via Chinese-mediated influence.
  • The Tripitaka Koreana (~1011–1087 CE, then 1236–1251 CE) — the complete Buddhist canon carved on 81,258 wooden printing blocks. Reads as an ATA-style cultural-preservation operation — information committed to a form designed to outlast political upheaval.

Joseon Dynasty (1392–1910 CE)

  • King Sejong (r. 1418–1450) — invented Hangul. ATA op. See Candidates.md.
  • Long period of relative internal stability with light agency intervention.
  • The Imjin War (1592–1598) — Japanese invasions of Korea. Significant operational period.

Modern Korea

  • Japanese annexation (1910–1945). Korean War (1950–1953). Division into North and South. South Korean democratization (1987). North Korean continuation as one of the world's most isolated states.
  • The contemporary North/South divide is a real-time philosophical conflict almost perfectly matching the larger ATA/EPOCH framing — it is not surprising that both agencies have substantial modern Korean operations.

Mongolia, Central Asia, and the Steppe

  • Periodic eruptions of steppe power (Xiongnu, Turks, Mongols, etc.) reshape Eurasian history on roughly thousand-year cycles. Some natural (the steppe is a real geopolitical engine), some EPOCH-influenced (the Mongol-attribution thread).
  • The Silk Road as a spatial bridge between EPOCH (Kunlun) and the western contested zones. Anchor infrastructure along the route is mutually competed for and occasionally jointly maintained when bandit threats exceed either side's tolerance.
  • Tibet itself — EPOCH's spatial origin. ATA has almost zero presence in Tibet across all eras. Operations there are treated as essentially attacking EPOCH's home, with corresponding extreme costs.

Southeast Asia and the Maritime World

Currently undersketched. Major candidate operational regions when development continues:

  • Angkor (Khmer Empire, ~9th–15th c.) — institutional complexity comparable to contemporary China; agency attribution open.
  • Maritime Southeast Asia — Srivijaya, Majapahit, etc. as trade-network operational space.
  • Vietnam — successive resistance against Chinese expansion. Long ATA presence possible (defense of regional autonomy fits ATA institutional-pluralism pattern).

Open Questions

  • How dense is ATA's Tang-era Chinese embed network actually? \"Sparse\" is the answer for most East Asian eras, but Tang is contested-favorable enough that ATA may have invested more here than the general regional pattern.
  • Was there ever a third East Asian agency operating from a hypothetical local shard? See ../GM-Secrets/Other-Shards.md. If yes, much of what currently reads as \"natural\" East Asian history might be a third party's work.
  • EPOCH's modern East Asian operations — how do they interact with the present-day Chinese, Japanese, and Korean governments? This is critical for any modern-era campaign in the region.
  • The Korean DMZ as a modern-era operational interface. Worth its own treatment.
