Doc

The agency's chief geneticist. The woman who invented modern infusion. A bootstrap paradox in human form.

Cover Identity (Young Doc — Modern Era)

A late-20s / early-30s former pharmaceutical researcher whose career imploded publicly when she self-injected an unapproved retroviral therapy in front of journalists during a regulatory hearing. The footage went viral. Her project was cancelled. She was dismissed from her role and quietly blacklisted across the industry.

Her real name is classified to all but a small circle inside ATA — see ../Core-Setting/Aliases-and-Identity.md. Within the agency she is Doc. The cover she maintains in the outside world (when one is needed) is built on the public-record version of her story: a disgraced biotech researcher trying to rebuild her life, working contract jobs, no longer in the public eye.

The cover does not need to hide much. Most of the world forgot her within a year of the scandal. She is, paradoxically, hidden by being a minor footnote.

True Identity

Doc is the inventor of the modern infusion protocol — the multi-month retroviral-accelerated procedure that lets agents become operational in months rather than the years required by the agency's legacy direct-exposure technique. Without her work, modern ATA would still be recruiting at pre-1940s scale: a handful of new agents per decade, severe selection bottlenecks, agency growth permanently capped.

She is also one of the only living examples of retroviral self-modification at full saturation. Her body has been continuously expressing the modified retrovirus for years. The effects are real, visible, and not entirely intended.

And there is a second version of her at Atlantis already, who has been there since the 1940s, and who knows exactly what is going to happen to Young Doc and when. See Two Versions below.

Backstory (Young Doc)

Trained as a pharmaceutical geneticist. Driven, brilliant, dangerously impatient. Spent most of her career developing a retroviral gene-therapy approach to [a degenerative disease — exact identity TBD by GM; suggested: a relative had ALS or Huntington's, providing the personal-motivation engine for her recklessness].

Working primarily alone — partly to maintain control of the project, partly because she was already cutting corners regulators would not have approved. She used her own DNA as the base for the retrovirus, on the grounds that genetic-identity matching at the source would prevent the immune-rejection failure modes her previous formulations had encountered.

The decision was operationally correct: the retrovirus did perform better in primate trials than any of her competitors' equivalents. It was also ethically catastrophic: she rushed toward human trials without authorization, regulators caught up with her, and the entire program was suspended pending review.

At the public hearing, accused of unsafe practice, she did the worst thing she could have done: she drew a syringe of her own formulation and injected herself in front of the cameras. To prove it was safe. The footage played for a week. The scientific community was horrified. Her project was definitively killed; she was dismissed.

The injection worked. Better than she had any right to expect.

ATA was already watching. The agency's Reality Analysis division had flagged her research two years earlier as a possible source of next-generation infusion technique. Her public scandal made her available. Within weeks of her dismissal, an ATA recruiter approached her at a hotel bar in a city she didn't recognize and made an offer. She accepted.

Physical Modifications

Self-derived retrovirus + agency infusion protocol applied to a body already partly self-modified produces compounding effects:

  • Wound regeneration. Cuts close in hours rather than days. Bruises clear by the next morning. Broken bones knit in weeks rather than months. She is not invulnerable — sufficient trauma can still kill her — but she has an order-of-magnitude advantage in recovery time over standard humans.
  • Abnormally perfect skin. Continuous low-level cellular regeneration prevents the accumulation of micro-damage that produces normal aging texture. Her skin looks smoother than is plausible for her apparent age. She is beautiful but the beauty is off — uncanny-valley territory. The cosmetic effect is significant enough that she applies subtle artificial imperfections (faint makeup, slightly disheveled hair) when she needs to pass unobtrusively.
  • Permanent apparent age in the early thirties. She does not age from time. She may eventually die from accumulated trauma, unrepairable degeneration, or accident — she is not immortal — but the calendar does not catch her. Permanently looks ~32.
  • Standard infusion effects on top. Once ATA completed her infusion protocol (her self-administered retrovirus actually accelerated the process — she stabilized in two months rather than the four her own invention would normally require), she gained the full standard agent package: agent-sense signature, memory privilege, slow-aging (already redundant for her), latent psi activation pathway.

Two Versions

Major campaign-structural reveal. GM-sensitive.

There are two versions of Doc at Atlantis.

Young Doc is the modern-era recruit. Player character. Just joined the agency. Has all of the above modifications. Looks early 30s. Is, biologically and chronologically, early 30s.

Old Doc is the head of I-6 Genetics Lab. She has been at Atlantis since the late 1930s, when she arrived from the modern era via a campaign-event time-jump (details to be played out — the loop closes when Young Doc, late in her campaign service, takes the trip back). Subjectively she is ~120 years old. She looks almost identical to Young Doc.

The difference is detectable but subtle:

  • Her demeanor. She is calm in the way of someone who has watched a lot of things happen and not been surprised in decades. Young Doc is reckless; Old Doc is patient.
  • Her eyes. Look at someone who has had 120 years of practice watching other people. You can tell. There is a depth in the gaze that is not consistent with the face it sits in.
  • Her voice. Slightly lower register than Young Doc (the vocal folds have actually slowly thickened over her century of life despite the regeneration — small biological details still drift). Her speech patterns are also from a different era; she still uses turns of phrase that aged out of common American English in the 1960s.

The other PCs do not know they are working with two versions of the same person. The reveal is a major campaign beat. See Encounter Notes below.

Operational Roles

Young Doc — Field Medic and PC

  • The team's medical capability. Trauma care, surgical improvisation, drug-and-poison knowledge, biological-hazard handling.
  • Her own regeneration is a tactical asset. Wounds that would benchsidekick another PC are temporary for her.
  • Genetic insight. She recognizes engineered biology when she sees it. Helen-tier glamour-psi, EPOCH biotech samples, myth-engineered assets — she gets more from inspection than the average agent.
  • Mostly normal social cover. She can pass as a contemporary American woman with minor cosmetic adjustment. Her cover identity is plausibly maintainable.

Old Doc — Head of the Genetics Lab

  • Runs I-6. All bio-research, all infusion protocols, all EPOCH-biotech analysis, all engineered-psi induction. She is the senior authority on the agency's most foundational technical capability.
  • Senior leadership council member. Coequal with Chief in agency-strategy decisions about anything bio-related.
  • The Cumaean precog's lab partner. Old Doc collaborates with the captured Cumaean (see ../Myth-Engineering/Oracle-Program.md) on engineered-psi research. They are the longest-serving cross-disciplinary research pair in modern ATA.
  • Mentor / handler-from-a-distance. Old Doc rarely deploys to the field. She handles in-facility consultations, post-mission medical reviews, and the occasional cryptic conversation with PCs that the team can't quite parse the meaning of.

Doc's Contribution: The Modern Infusion Protocol

Before Doc, agency infusion was a multi-year process with >90% washout. The legacy technique relied on prolonged proximity to refined fuel and gradual tissue saturation through passive exposure — extremely slow, extremely high-failure, biologically punishing. The agency's pre-1940 recruitment rate was a handful of agents per decade per region.

Doc's retroviral acceleration changed everything. The protocol she developed (now canonical in Orichalcum.md):

  • Phase 1 — Acclimation (existed before her). Trace meteor exposure for several weeks.
  • Phase 2 — Loading (her invention). The retroviral component actively pulls refined orichalcum powder into tissue saturation. Cuts the loading time from years to ~3 months.
  • Phase 3 — Stabilization (existed before her). Biology equilibrates with the meteor matter.

Without Doc's Phase 2, modern ATA does not have a modern recruitment pipeline. Without a recruitment pipeline, the agency cannot field enough agents to contest EPOCH at the operational tempo the campaign assumes. She is structurally the most important single individual in modern ATA's existence. Even Chief acknowledges this.

The protocol's existence is the bootstrap paradox at character scale. Doc was recruited because Doc invented the recruitment process that made her recruitment efficient enough for ATA to bother with her. The loop closes through her: Young Doc goes back, becomes Old Doc, invents the protocol, enables Young Doc's recruitment.

What She Knows / Refuses to Discuss

Young Doc

  • Her own genome and retroviral mechanism in technical detail.
  • Her motivation — the relative whose disease drove her career, who probably died despite the rushed research.
  • What ATA does and what it is — she has been read into the agency's basic operations.
  • What she does NOT know — that there is an older version of her at Atlantis, that she will eventually become that older version, that she invented the infusion protocol. The campaign's central character mystery for her.

Old Doc

  • Everything Young Doc knows, plus 120 years of subsequent service.
  • The Two Versions secret. She has been preparing for Young Doc's recruitment for decades. She knows what is about to happen and when.
  • Why she went back. The specific event (campaign-late) that made her take the trip. She refuses to discuss it with anyone, especially Young Doc, because telling Young Doc would alter the cascade. Causality protection.
  • What lies on the other side of the trip back. What happened to her parents (who died of old age while she was at Atlantis from the 1940s onward, with no idea what had happened to their daughter). What happened to the people she knew before recruitment. The relative whose disease she failed to cure (now long dead). These are unbearable topics and she has long since stopped acknowledging them in conversation.

Tells and Vulnerabilities

Young Doc

  • Skin uncanny-valley. Detectable on close inspection. Trained observers (other agents, EPOCH operatives, sharp civilians) notice. The cosmetic mitigation works in casual settings; under bright light or HD camera, it fails.
  • Regeneration as a tell. A wound she takes in front of strangers heals visibly within minutes. She has to fake injury severity in front of mundane witnesses — appear more hurt than she is, then "recover" at a plausible rate.
  • Public record. Her self-injection footage exists on the open internet. Anyone who searches the right keywords can find a younger version of her face. She manages this by maintaining a controlled visual signature (different hair, different makeup, different posture) that defeats casual facial recognition. EPOCH counter-intelligence with serious resources would defeat the mitigation.
  • Personal motivation as leverage. The relative she failed to save is a permanent emotional vulnerability. EPOCH interrogators who learn about that history have a powerful pressure point.

Old Doc

  • Same skin uncanny-valley.
  • Her eyes. The depth-of-experience gap is not maskable. Other agents who interact with her closely sense it.
  • Her demeanor reveals tenure. She speaks slowly, hesitates rarely, never seems surprised. Long-service agents recognize the pattern in themselves and in her.
  • Her continued existence. If EPOCH ever identified Doc as the inventor of the modern infusion protocol, they would target her for retroactive elimination at her pre-recruitment moment — before her public self-injection, ideally. That would erase modern ATA's recruitment pipeline. Old Doc's real identity is one of the agency's most-protected secrets, on par with Chief's.

The Doc–Doc Loop

The reveal that Young Doc and Old Doc are the same person is among the campaign's biggest character beats. Suggested handling:

  1. Foreshadowing. Throughout the campaign, drop hints: Old Doc avoids being in the same room as Young Doc; senior leadership treats them with unusual care when both are around; small moments where Old Doc says something that only Young Doc would understand and Young Doc almost catches it.
  2. Player-side coordination. The player playing Young Doc is in on the secret from session one. They know they will become Old Doc. The roleplay choice: how does that knowledge shape Young Doc's behavior? Does she feel destined? Confined? Resigned? Determined?
  3. The Other Players figure it out. Probably mid-late campaign. Either through deliberate investigation (PCs piecing together the tells) or accidental discovery (Old Doc lets something slip; Young Doc encounters a journal she shouldn't have; etc.).
  4. The Loop Closure. Late-campaign event sends Young Doc back to the 1930s/40s. She begins her century of service. She becomes Old Doc. The player keeps playing the same character. The remaining campaign features Old Doc as PC, no longer in the field but still operationally significant from base.

Encounter Notes (For Players)

If Young Doc starts the campaign:

  • The player needs to know the Doc-Doc structure. This is not a surprise to play; it's a structure to roleplay through.
  • Other players should be kept in the dark, with the GM dropping hints as appropriate.
  • Old Doc, when she appears on-screen, should feel uncanny — familiar but wrong. The other PCs should feel there's something odd about her without being able to place it.

If Old Doc is the visible figure first (campaign starts before any of the PCs are Young Doc):

  • Reveal Young Doc's recruitment late, after the players already know Old Doc as the lab head.
  • The reveal lands as: "She's that one. The teammate is her."

Pronouns and Presentation

Doc presents as female in both versions. Pronouns: she/her in modern operational use. Her real-name pre-recruitment identity is similarly female-presenting and consistent.

Open Questions

  • Specific disease she was researching. GM call. Suggested: ALS or Huntington's (degenerative, real-world plausible, the kind of thing where retroviral genetic editing is a real research direction). A personal connection (relative who had it) provides the emotional engine.
  • Her real name. Like Chief, never canonically revealed. The player playing Young Doc may know it; the agency archive has it; nobody else does. EPOCH never finds out, or the campaign ends in retroactive erasure.
  • The exact event that sends Young Doc back. Campaign-late. Probably a chosen sacrifice rather than an accident — a moment where Old Doc finally tells Young Doc the truth and Young Doc accepts the loop. Worth pre-planning.
  • Old Doc's century at Atlantis. What did she do for 80+ years between the 1940s arrival and the modern era? Probably: invented the protocol, then built the I-6 Genetics Lab from a small clinical operation into the full bio-research hub it is in 2026. The lab's evolution mirrors hers.
  • Doc's relationship with Chief. They are the two oldest founder-cohort-era members of modern ATA. They have worked together for ~80 years subjective time. Their relationship — professional, personal, possibly romantic at some point and now settled into something else — is rich material the GM can develop.
  • What happens if Young Doc dies before she becomes Old Doc? Paradox. Old Doc continues to exist (she has her own subjective continuity) but her past becomes... what? This is GM-territory and probably should be avoided as a campaign outcome rather than designed for.
