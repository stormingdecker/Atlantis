Major

The team's combat specialist. Twenty years in the US Army, the last decade training Afghan recruits. Watched it all collapse in 2021. ATA was watching when he walked away.

Cover Identity

A former US Army Special Forces officer, retired at the rank of Major after 20 years of service. Honorable discharge in 2022, six months after the fall of Kabul. Now in his early 40s. The cover identity is essentially his pre-recruitment life lightly fictionalized — he is publicly known to have served, publicly known to have specialized in foreign internal defense (training partner-nation forces in Afghanistan), and publicly known to have left the service shortly after the withdrawal.

What is not publicly known is what he has been doing in the 18 months between his discharge and his ATA recruitment — the cover story attributes this to "consulting for a private security contractor," which is plausible enough that nobody investigates.

His real name is in his pre-recruitment service records, which ATA has not destroyed — too many people in the system already knew him, and erasing those records would itself be a flag. Instead, the records are carefully preserved and the agency has worked to make his post-service trajectory boring and unremarkable. From the perspective of any modern investigator, Major is a slightly-decorated SF officer who retired, drifted into private contracting, and disappeared into the unmemorable middle of his profession.

True Identity

Within ATA he is Major — a callsign that doubles as his pre-recruitment rank, common enough to function as an alias without seeming like one. Per the aliases convention, his real name is operationally suppressed but not destroyed; the agency maintains it carefully because his pre-service-records present a structural defense against retroactive identification (any EPOCH investigation that targets his real name will hit the same army records that anyone else would find, with no flagged anomalies).

He is approximately 43 years old at the start of the campaign. Born in the early 1980s. Two divorces, two children he sees twice a year, a small house in central Virginia he rents to a Marine couple. He is fit, weathered, calm. He has been ready for a long time and is now ready for something different.

Backstory

Enlisted at 19 immediately after 9/11. Showed early aptitude — selected for SF assessment within four years, completed the SF qualification course in his early 20s. Speaks Pashto (operational fluency), Dari (functional), and basic Arabic.

The bulk of his career was Foreign Internal Defense (FID) — Special Forces' specialty in training partner-nation military forces. From 2009 onward, his rotations were almost exclusively in Afghanistan, embedded with Afghan National Army (ANA) units, training them to fight the Taliban independently.

He believed in the mission. He worked with the same Afghan officers for years, in some cases mentoring the same individuals across multiple rotations. He attended their weddings, met their families, lived in their towns. By 2018 his Pashto was good enough to argue in. By 2020 the ANA units he had trained were nominally functional.

In August 2021, when the US withdrawal began, the ANA collapsed in days. Cities he had spent years preparing fell to the Taliban in hours. Officers he had mentored were killed or fled with their families. The Afghan partners he had built the strongest relationships with — the ones he had personally vouched for — many of them did not make it to Kabul. The ones who did get to Kabul mostly did not make it onto the evacuation flights.

He was in the country during the final months. He helped evacuate civilians from HKIA. He watched the C-17 take off with people clinging to its landing gear. He came home and resigned his commission within six months.

The reasons he stayed in the service that long were now over. He could not articulate the reasons he had now, but they were not the same reasons.

ATA's Recruitment

ATA's modern-era talent-scouting had Major on its watch list since approximately 2018. The agency monitors specific career profiles: combat experience with long-arc partner-training emphasis, foreign-language fluency, demonstrated ideological complexity (people who have believed in a mission and seen it fail). Such recruits transition into ATA at unusually high success rates and have unusual durability under the agency's ideological strain.

Major was approached in late 2024 by an ATA recruiter (probably someone working a private-security cover identity). The recruiter did not pitch him on time travel or any of it. The recruiter pitched him on a specialized operations role: elite team, classified work, "the kind of mission you can believe in." He took the meeting because he didn't have anything better to do. He took the next meeting because the recruiter was unusually patient. He signed on because by the third meeting the recruiter was answering questions Major hadn't asked yet — questions about what he wanted from the next phase of his life — and the answers were correct.

He was put through onboarding in early 2025 and assigned to the prologue team. The prologue (see ../Campaigns/Arc-00-The-Cryo-Train.md) is his first ATA operation.

Capabilities

  • Tactical combat. Twenty years of professional soldiering. Small-arms expert; experienced with most modern military systems; trained in close-quarters battle, demolitions, sniping, and air-assault operations. He is the team's primary fighter.
  • Physical conditioning. Strong, fast, durable. The fittest member of the team by a significant margin. He can carry a teammate or significant equipment over long distances. He can fight through injuries that would put most agents down.
  • Small-unit leadership. Trained as an SF team leader. Used to making decisions under fire, coordinating multiple people across multiple roles. He is comfortable being the team's tactical lead during combat operations.
  • Training and instruction. His FID background means he can teach. As other PCs and recruits join the agency, Major often takes on informal training roles — teaching them weapons handling, tactical movement, basic combat survival. He is a multiplier for the team's overall capability.
  • Languages. Pashto, Dari, basic Arabic. Operational German (Army-school refresher). Acquires new languages slowly but durably through agency-assisted accelerated study.
  • Calm under pressure. Sustained combat exposure has produced the trained calm of someone who has been very afraid many times and learned to function through it. The other PCs notice this immediately. It is grounding.

After infusion (post-prologue), he gains standard agent capabilities: agent-sense signature, memory privilege, slow-aging, the orichalcum infusion-related capability extensions. His pre-existing physical conditioning combines well with infused durability; he becomes one of the agency's most physically formidable agents within months.

Operational Role

  • Tactical lead in field operations. When combat is imminent, Major calls the play. The team defers to him on tactics; he defers to them on specialties.
  • Heavy lifting. He carries the team's heaviest equipment without complaint. In Bronze Age and other historical missions where physical labor matters, his strength is a major asset.
  • Trainer. When new agents join the agency, Major often pulls light training duties. He has agency-wide informal reputation as a good instructor — patient, demanding, fair.
  • Bodyguard / extraction muscle. If a mission requires extracting someone under fire, Major is the one carrying them. The math of who-can-protect-who is unambiguous in his case.

What He Knows / Refuses to Discuss

  • His own military service in detail. He will talk about it sparingly and in operational terms. He does not romanticize it.
  • The names of his Afghan partners. Many of them are dead. He carries a list of names in a notebook he reads each morning. He will not share the list with the team. The names are personal.
  • His family. Two ex-wives (divorced amicably from both, no ongoing tension). Two children (a daughter, 14; a son, 11), both with his second ex-wife. He sees them on agency-coordinated visit windows. He misses them. He does not talk about them unprompted.
  • What he refuses to discuss publicly. His private grief about Afghanistan. His private guilt about the partners he could not get out. His private opinions about the policymakers who made the decisions that led to August 2021. These exist; he keeps them inside.

Tells and Vulnerabilities

  • Military bearing. He walks like a soldier. His posture, the way he scans a room when he enters it, the way he positions himself to face the door — all are obvious to other people with military training. In civilian-cover situations he has to consciously sit not facing the door, which he can do but which is uncomfortable.
  • Pashto language tells. Under stress, he sometimes counts in Pashto rather than English. Sometimes mutters curses. Most people don't notice. Pashto-speakers (rare in agency contexts) would.
  • Old knee injury. Bone-graft repair from 2015, mostly fine, but stiff in cold weather. Long mountain climbs aggravate it. He uses ibuprofen and doesn't complain. The agency's infusion-era healing has improved it significantly.
  • The Afghanistan trauma. Real, deep, exploitable. EPOCH interrogators who learn his background would target the trauma. He has been through agency-provided trauma counseling and has tools, but the wound is permanent. Do not casually invoke Afghanistan in mission contexts with him present unless you mean it.
  • The family pressure point. His children's well-being is a leverage point. EPOCH knowing about his children is a campaign-significant danger; their existence is part of why his real identity is one of ATA's protected secrets, even though the records are preserved.

Encounter Notes (For Players)

Major is quiet and patient. He listens more than he talks. He observes the team's dynamics for the first few missions before he starts contributing tactical opinions; once he starts, the team realizes he has been silently calibrating everyone's capabilities the whole time.

He is not a hardass. He is firm but kind. He gives the team's less-experienced members real coaching during downtime. He stays late at base to spot people doing PT in the gym (O-5 Commons recreation suites). He is the team's older sibling without being the team's father — he carries his weight, he doesn't moralize, he is present in a way most of the team isn't.

He is also processing throughout the early campaign. The discovery that time travel is real and that ATA is fighting a war against a future agency is a significant recontextualization of his career and his disillusionment. He sits with it. He talks to Doc about it once or twice. He doesn't share his processing with the team broadly. By the mid-campaign he is settled into the agency; the trauma is not gone, but the purpose is back.

If a player wants their Major to be more loquacious, that's fine — these are roleplay defaults, not requirements. But the quiet competent strength default works well for the team's dynamic and is consistent with the character's biography.

Pronouns and Presentation

Major presents as male. Pronouns: he/him. Cisgender, straight, mid-American (Virginia/West Virginia accent that occasionally drops "y'all" when he's tired).

Open Questions

  • His real name. Per the aliases convention, never revealed in the campaign. The agency archives have it; the players' characters do not need to know it.
  • The unit he served with. Suggested: 5th Special Forces Group (Fort Campbell), which has historic Afghanistan focus. GM may pick a different SF group if relevant.
  • The Afghan partners on his list. Each name is a potential NPC who can be the focus of an arc — either a successful extraction (someone he gets to save, late in the campaign, via an ATA modern-era op) or a confirmation of fate (someone whose death he learns the details of, with associated grief).
  • His children's mother and her own awareness. The ex-wife knows nothing about ATA. The agency has been working to ensure she doesn't notice anything strange about his current "private contracting" work.
  • His relationship with Chief. They are both former military, both ideologically complicated, both processing past institutional disillusionment. They will recognize something in each other quickly. Whether they become close, professionally trusting, or wary is up to GM and player development.
  • Late-campaign role. Major is the team's combat lead. As the campaign progresses and the team's overall combat capability grows (infusion, orichalcum-mark gear), Major's relative advantage shrinks. Does he transition into a senior training role? A field commander role? An advisor role to a new generation of recruits? Worth thinking about by mid-campaign.
