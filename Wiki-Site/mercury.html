Mercury

The aviatrix who was never supposed to land. Extracted from the Pacific, 1937. ATA's "anything that moves" specialist.

Cover Identity

She was, before her preservation, Amelia Mary Earhart — American aviator, lecturer, author, and the most famous woman pilot of the early 20th century. Born Atchison, Kansas, July 24, 1897. First woman to fly the Atlantic solo (1932). First person of either sex to fly solo from Honolulu to Oakland (1935). Multiple altitude and speed records on the Lockheed Vega and the Pilcairn autogiro. Public face of women's aviation and an aggressive advocate for women in technical fields.

The historical record about her is substantially accurate up to the moment of her disappearance. Her childhood in Kansas, her medical-aide work in Toronto during the influenza pandemic, her social work at Denison House in Boston, her marriage to George Putnam, her two terms on the Purdue University faculty, her partnership with Fred Noonan on the round-the-world flight — all real, all documented, all hers. The fiction's only embellishments are the conspiracy theories that bloomed after Howland: the Saipan capture stories, the Nikumaroro castaway theories, the "Tokyo Rose was actually Amelia" nonsense. All of it is wrong. The truth is stranger.

Within the agency she is Mercury — operational callsign, in continuous use since her recruitment. Selected for the obvious mythological reasons (messenger of the gods, traveler between worlds, patron of crossings) and because it deflects the awkwardness of using her real name in mission radio chatter. Casual nickname: Em (from "M" for Mercury, also a childhood family nickname her sister Muriel actually used — see What She Won't Discuss).

Her real birth name — the historical "Amelia Earhart" — is classified, per the agency's standard aliases convention. Inside ATA archives, her pre-recruitment identity is preserved (she is, after all, a documented historical figure under her real name) but operationally always referred to as Mercury.

True Identity

Same person. There is no separate "true identity" hidden under the Amelia Earhart name. She is not a long-term cover or an embedded persona — she is a real person extracted at the end of her real life, given a callsign for operational convenience.

Critically, she is not a natural psi. Unlike Sigerson, who was recruited because of his precog, Mercury was recruited for a more prosaic and more practical reason: cross-era vehicle aptitude is the rarest skill set in the agency, and the people who have it are almost always already dead by the time ATA could plausibly extract them. Earhart fit a narrow operational profile: a known historical figure whose canonical death was clean, instrumented, and isolated; a documented operator of cutting-edge (for her era) aerial technology; a person with the temperament to absorb being uprooted from her own timeline.

ATA had been tracking her for over a decade before Howland. Her file in the Reality Analysis archives shows entries dating to 1928 (after the Friendship transatlantic flight that made her famous). ATA's standing principle is that visible historical figures are expensive to extract — every preserved name creates downstream causality management problems — and Earhart's recruitment was debated for years. The deciding factor: the Howland leg of the round-the-world flight was known to fail. The wreck was unrecoverable. The bodies were unrecoverable. There would be no awkward forensic anomaly if the extraction succeeded, because there was already no forensic evidence at all in the public record. It was a "perfect" extraction window — the kind ATA's exfiltration planners describe as "history did the cover-up for us."

The agency's recruitment doctrine prefers natural psis, but accepts mundane high-performers when the skill set is hard to replicate and the extraction window is clean. Mercury is the textbook case of the second category.

The Howland Operation

The disappearance of Amelia Earhart and Fred Noonan on July 2, 1937, near Howland Island in the central Pacific — somewhere between 19:12 and 20:14 UTC — was the exfiltration event. The public-record narrative — radio messages growing frantic as fuel ran low, the Itasca's loop receiver failing to fix the Electra's position, the eventual silence and the largest naval search in American history producing nothing — is what actually happened on the historical record's side of the operation. ATA did not stage the failure; the failure was real. ATA capitalized on it.

What actually happened: the Lockheed Electra 10E was, by ~20:00 UTC, genuinely out of fuel and in genuine final descent. Noonan's celestial navigation had been compromised by inadequate maps and an unfamiliar chronometer. The Itasca's direction-finding equipment was operating on a frequency the Electra could not transmit on. They were not going to find Howland. They were not going to find any island. They were going to ditch in deep ocean and die — Earhart possibly on impact, Noonan from injury or exposure or sharks within the day.

An ATA extraction team intercepted the Electra at approximately 20:11 UTC, three minutes before it would have ditched. The operation:

  • Anchor placement. ATA had positioned a temporal anchor on a hidden support vessel near the Phoenix Islands two weeks earlier, masked as a private yacht. The anchor was tuned to a narrow extraction corridor through which the Electra's projected flight path would briefly pass.
  • Transit team. A four-person ATA team boost-transited from the 1980s to the anchor (1937), then deployed a smaller two-person extraction craft built on a captured EPOCH lift-platform design.
  • Intercept. The extraction craft matched the Electra's altitude and speed (~140 mph at ~1000 feet, a hard but achievable pickup given the period-appropriate stealth profile) and pulled both occupants and a small portion of recoverable equipment off the aircraft. Earhart was conscious and combative; Noonan was semi-conscious from a concussion sustained during turbulence over the previous hour.
  • Disposal. The Electra was allowed to descend and ditch as planned. The wreck went into deep water in the central Pacific, where it remains, where no recovery effort has ever found it, and where ATA's gradient-screening has actively discouraged later well-resourced search expeditions.
  • Extraction. Earhart and Noonan were transited to the 1980s anchor for medical stabilization, then brought forward to the agency's modern facility for full revival and integration.

The operation cost ATA significant resources — period-appropriate stealth aircraft are expensive to maintain, and the boost-transit budget for a four-person team plus extraction craft is non-trivial — but it was rated successful on every metric ATA's after-action protocols evaluate.

Earhart was conscious throughout the extraction. This is unusual for ATA recoveries (most extractees are unconscious by the time the team reaches them) and shaped the rest of her integration. Her first words to the extraction lead, reportedly: "I don't know who you are or what this aircraft is, but I have a co-pilot and you are taking him too."

Fred Noonan

Mercury's central unresolved thread.

Frederick Joseph Noonan survived the extraction itself but did not survive the next 48 hours. The concussion he sustained on the Electra before extraction was more severe than the extraction team's field-medicine could manage in 1937, and the boost-transit to the 1980s anchor — which agency protocols normally only attempt with patients stabilized to specific neurological baselines — was performed on him as an emergency measure to access modern medical care faster. He died en route to the 1980s anchor, of complications consistent with secondary intracranial bleeding, before reaching the medical facility that could have saved him.

ATA's after-action report classifies his death as operational unavoidable. The extraction team had two choices: take both occupants and risk losing Noonan to the transit, or take only Earhart and let Noonan die in the ditching. They took both. Noonan died anyway. The agency owns this honestly.

Mercury knows. She was told during her own revival, by the same agent who handled her initial briefing. She accepted the answer professionally and has not asked again. She has, on agency records, made one request: that if ATA ever recovers technology that would allow safer extraction of complex injuries, she be informed, and the question of an alternative-timeline Noonan recovery be revisited. The request is filed but not actively pursued; the gradient mechanics that would make such an operation possible do not currently exist.

GM hooks for Noonan:

  • The "safer extraction" technology could become a campaign goal. EPOCH may have it. Recovering it from EPOCH could open the door to a retroactive Noonan rescue — a personal arc for Mercury that the players could choose to pursue or not.
  • A counter-narrative: EPOCH, learning of ATA's interest in Noonan, could fabricate a story that they recovered him themselves and have him operational in the 2200s. This would be a precision attack on Mercury's psychological stability and a recurring intelligence-warfare hook.
  • A long-tail mystery: Noonan's family, in the historical record, accepted his death along with Earhart's. If ATA's archives reveal that his body was recovered (in the agency's care) and buried in a marked location, that fact is itself operationally sensitive — surviving descendants do not know it.

Training and Integration (1937–present)

Mercury was revived at an ATA facility in northern California in late 1937 (subjective time for her — gradient-equivalent to 1986 in the modern record, given the boost-transit). Her initial integration:

  • First six months: medical stabilization (the boost-transit was rough on her too — minor decompression-equivalent damage that modern medicine cleared cleanly), psychological intake (handled by an agency specialist who had worked with prior period-extractees, including some of Helen's contemporaries who had been recovered earlier), and read-in to the full agency framework.
  • Infusion: completed in 1938 (subjective). She was a clean candidate — physically healthy, no addiction history, no genetic complications — and the protocols of the era worked smoothly on her. She is now a standard infused agent: agent-sense, slow-aging, mild regeneration, the full package.
  • Forward training: the agency walked her through 50 years of aviation evolution in roughly three years of subjective time. She trained on warbird trainers (P-51, T-6), then early jets (T-33, F-86), then commercial aircraft (727, then 747), then modern fighters (F-15, F-16, briefly F-22), then helicopters (UH-1, then full rotary-wing certifications across modern types). The agency's aviation training cell is one of its larger investments precisely because Mercury demonstrated the model worked.
  • Backward training: less time-intensive but more diverse. She learned cavalry mounted-riding (her childhood horseback experience was a useful baseline), age-of-sail rigging, late-19th-century early-automotive driving, balloon piloting, late-medieval/early-modern carriage and wagon handling, and a number of niche skills (camel, elephant, dog sled) for specific mission scenarios.
  • Operational deployment: her first field mission was in 1942 (subjective ~1991), as a co-pilot on an extraction in late-Victorian London. She has been operationally active ever since. Her subjective age is now ~90, biological age locked by infusion at ~39, chronological age (counting cryo and gradient transit) ~129. She does not enjoy thinking about that math.

Capabilities (Post-Revival, Current)

  • Vehicle mastery — modern. Fixed-wing (light through commercial through fighter), rotary-wing (light through heavy lift), high-performance automobile, powerboat, jetski, snowmobile. She holds (under ATA cover identities) certified ratings in most of these. Specialty: extraction-tier flying — getting an aircraft into and out of a difficult site under hostile conditions.
  • Vehicle mastery — historical. Age-of-sail rigging (basic crew, not full master mariner), horseback (well above average; she rides cleanly and at speed), period automobiles (1900s through modern), carriages and wagons, balloons, biplanes (she trained on her own type originally), early-jet experimental aircraft. She is not a master of every historical mode but she is at least competent in nearly all of them.
  • Mechanical / engineering intuition. Trained as an aircraft mechanic before flying. She can diagnose a mechanical failure quickly, improvise repairs from limited materials, and operate machinery she has never seen before by reasoning from first principles.
  • Navigation. Trained backward to celestial and dead-reckoning; trained forward to GPS, INS, and modern integrated navigation systems. Specialty: rapid-reorientation in unfamiliar terrain or seas, including dense weather.
  • Public-presence experience. She lived for a decade as one of the most-photographed women in America. She is comfortable in front of cameras, in crowds, in high-profile situations. This is useful for cover identities that require visible roles (test pilot, technical lecturer, expedition leader).
  • Standard agent capabilities from infusion: agent-sense signature, memory privilege, slow-aging, mild regeneration.

Limitations and Adjustments

  • No psi. She has no precog, no agent-sense beyond the standard infused baseline, no anomalous capabilities. She is a trained mundane in an agency that increasingly relies on psi assets, and she is candid about this gap. It does not bother her; she leans into being the "normal one."
  • Modern combat. Light arms training only. She can carry a sidearm and use it competently in self-defense. She is not a shooter, not a hand-to-hand combat specialist, not a tactical operator. On combat missions she is in the vehicle, not in the breach.
  • Period combat. Knows basic horseback weapons handling (sabre, pistol from saddle) and minimal small-arms competence for early-20th-century scenarios. Comparable to her modern combat baseline.
  • Languages. English (native), Spanish (working — picked up in her aviation career), French (working — she lived briefly in Paris), German (passable — agency training), Mandarin (basic — agency training, still ongoing). She is not a linguist; she leans on translators and tech.
  • Cultural displacement. Real but less acute than Sigerson's. She was awake during her transition (Sigerson was frozen), she has had ~50 subjective years to acclimate, and the 1930s-to-2020s cultural distance is much narrower than the 1890s-to-2020s gap. She still has occasional moments — particular slang she does not parse, certain technology she finds genuinely beautiful in a way that mortifies her teammates, an enduring discomfort with smartphones held vertically for video.
  • Public-figure caution. Her face is well-documented. ATA maintains careful management of her in-public appearances — she does not attend events likely to draw aviation-history enthusiasts, she does not allow herself to be photographed with the agency cover identities under conditions that could be reverse-searched, and her operational deployments are screened against the risk of being seen by someone who recognizes her. This is the dominant operational constraint on her use.
  • Survivor's-guilt patterns. Mostly resolved through agency counseling but not absent. Noonan, her mother (who outlived her in the historical record by twenty-five years), her sister Muriel, her husband George (who searched for her unsuccessfully for years). She has compartmentalized well; the compartments occasionally leak.

Operational Role

  • Lead pilot, extraction operations. Mercury is the agency's most-requested extraction-vehicle operator. If a mission needs a hard landing in a hostile environment and a fast departure, she is the first call. She has personally flown extractions in every decade since 1942 subjective, across every continent, under conditions ranging from civilian airfield through clandestine forward strip through "she landed it on a road."
  • Vehicle training cadre. She is one of the agency's principal trainers for new agent vehicle-skills onboarding. Almost every infused agent who has joined since the 1960s has logged at least some training hours with her. This makes her one of the most widely-known senior agents in ATA's day-to-day culture — most operatives have met her, many owe her a favor or three.
  • Historical-era transit consultant. When a mission plan requires period-appropriate vehicle handling (a horse-drawn extraction in 1840s London, a ship-to-shore landing in age-of-sail conditions, a biplane chase in 1920s Africa), Mercury is consulted both for the planning and often for the execution. She enjoys these missions; they let her use skills she would otherwise rust on.
  • Limited senior consulting role. She is not a strategic advisor in the way Chief or Sigerson are. She knows her lane and she stays in it. She will offer opinions when asked and otherwise defers to the leadership cohort.

What She Knows

  • Her own history. All of it. She is, after all, a documented historical figure with extensive contemporaneous records (her own books, her husband's promotional materials, the press archives of her decade).
  • Aviation history from her era through the present, in operational depth. Aircraft, engines, navigation systems, training programs, key figures, the evolution of the field. She is the agency's best resource for this entire technical lineage.
  • Period (1920s–30s) American social and political context in operational detail. Useful for mission deployments to her own era.
  • What ATA tells her about the modern campaign. She is fully read in.

What She Won't Discuss

  • George. She loved her husband. He loved her. She watched, from the agency's archives, his decade of search efforts, his eventual remarriage in 1939, his death in 1950. She does not discuss him.
  • Muriel. Her sister outlived her in the historical record until 1998 — comfortably within the window where ATA could have arranged a meeting under a covered identity. The agency offered. Mercury refused. She has not explained the refusal except to say, once, "She had a life to live without me in it. It would have been cruel."
  • The conspiracy theories about her disappearance. She read them all in the first year after her revival. She found most of them upsetting (especially the Japanese-capture narratives, which she considers libelous toward Japan), some of them merely silly. She does not discuss them, makes no effort to correct them, and asks that the agency not raise them in her presence.
  • The Electra. She loved that aircraft. It is at the bottom of the central Pacific. She would prefer it stay there.

Tells and Vulnerabilities

  • Recognizable face. Even more than Sigerson's. She is one of the most-photographed women of the 20th century, and her face is in every aviation museum, every history textbook, every American public-school curriculum. Modern facial-recognition against the Smithsonian archive will match her. ATA's management of her public exposure is the dominant tradecraft constraint on her deployment.
  • Period-coded mannerisms (mild). She has worked hard to modernize her speech and gestures and is largely successful. She still has occasional tells: she holds a cigarette in a way that has been out of fashion since the 1950s (she has quit smoking but the gesture appears under stress), she addresses servers as "miss" or "sir" in a register that registers as old-fashioned in modern American English, she occasionally lapses into the slightly performative phrasing of 1930s public-speaking style.
  • No psi defenses beyond infusion baseline. A skilled hostile psi could read her, push her, manipulate her with much less resistance than the agency's psi-trained senior staff would offer. She knows this and accepts agency protective measures (psi-shielding amulets, periodic psi-defense training) but the underlying vulnerability remains.
  • George / Muriel as emotional vector. If EPOCH ever discovered the emotional weight she places on the relationships she left behind and weaponized it (a fabricated communication purporting to be from a recovered George, a constructed scenario where Muriel was somehow recovered), they could damage her badly. ATA flags this in her file as a known risk.
  • Aircraft-loss trauma vulnerability. She has logged ~50 subjective years of flying without losing an aircraft in her care. The agency's medical staff assess that the first time she does — and it will happen, statistically — she will need significant support to return to operational status.

Pronouns and Presentation

Mercury presents as female. Pronouns: she/her. Her pre-extraction historical identity also presented as female and used she/her pronouns. She dresses modern-casual in the agency facility (trousers and a button-down, often a flight jacket — habits that long predate fashion); in cover identities she dresses to the cover.

Encounter Notes (For Players)

Players will almost certainly meet Mercury early — she is the agency's standing pilot-instructor, and any PC who flies anything will train with her at some point. She is approachable, professional, and warm in a slightly reserved way.

  • She is competent. She walks into a problem, assesses it, and starts solving it without performing the assessment. Players will sometimes miss that she has already understood a situation because she does not narrate her own thinking.
  • She is patient. She trains new pilots without condescension. She has trained four generations of agents and she will train the players' generation the same way.
  • She is funny in a dry, understated, midwestern way. Most of her jokes are about pilots, aircraft, or the absurdity of agency bureaucracy.
  • She is protective of her vehicles and her crews. If a mission plan endangers her aircraft or her co-pilot unnecessarily, she will say so plainly. She will fly the mission anyway if the agency commits to it, but the leadership knows when she has objected.
  • She is not a strategist or a politician. Players who try to involve her in agency politics will find her gracefully extracting herself.
  • She does not talk about her own history unless asked, and even when asked she keeps it brief. Players who want to know her well will have to earn the access over time.

If a mission needs vehicle-skills tutoring during play (a PC needs to fly something they have not been trained on), Mercury is the GM's default cutscene presence. Two hours with her, narrated briefly, and the PC can attempt the skill at appropriate difficulty.

Open Questions

  • The Noonan thread. Whether or not to pursue the alternative-extraction storyline. Major personal arc for Mercury if pursued. Skip cleanly if not.
  • Public exposure incident. Sooner or later a player will deploy with Mercury into a situation where she is recognized. How that goes — what cover story holds, what happens if it doesn't — is a recurring potential complication worth pre-thinking.
  • Whether she has any unrevealed psi capability. Canon: she does not. GM may decide otherwise if it suits the campaign (e.g., a latent ability that emerges decades into her infusion). Not recommended in early arcs; reserves better for a major reveal.
  • Aircraft loss. When it happens, what aircraft, with what consequences. Worth pre-thinking as a recurring possibility rather than a one-off shock.
  • The "extracted-at-death" pattern as agency doctrine. Mercury joins Sigerson as the second documented case. If the GM commits to this as ATA's standard recruitment doctrine for visible historical figures, an entire roster of additional candidates opens up (see Candidates.md). Worth a session-zero conversation about how often this pattern recurs.
