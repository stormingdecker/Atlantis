Anachrotech — the Craft of Luminous Artifacts

The craft layer beneath the artifact-reframing principle: how future-made objects are built, why they look like magic, and how they become persistent player upgrades. Where Overview.md covers the reward economy and Candidates.md rosters specific legends, this page is the materials-and-power grammar that any artifact can be assembled from.

This page covers the legendary tier — the arc-defining, gold-rarity artifacts. For EPOCH's standard field issue — the repeatable mid-tier "boss-drop" loot from beating their agents — see EPOCH-Kit.md.

Anachrotech (agency-clinical) = an object made with technology from a future that has no business existing in the era it surfaces in. To the culture that finds it, it is magic, a miracle, a divine relic, an enchanted blade. Those folk readings are not wrong — they are a plain description of the craft, distorted by people with no vocabulary for it.

The one principle: the shard does everything

The heart of luminous anachrotech is a single design fact:

A piece of the meteor, embedded in the object, is simultaneously its power source, its heat, its light, and its beacon — and there is no way to separate those. No wiring, no switch, no electronics. It is exotic matter, always on.

This is the same mechanism as the Antikythera device: an embedded Origin (or Kunlun) fragment emits the parent meteor's characteristic radiance and decay heat at micro-scale — enough to power the object's function indefinitely, with no external fuel. The device is always ~38–40°C, faintly luminous, and — critically — detectable.

What Orichalcum.md established for agents and gear, luminous artifacts push to the visible extreme:

  • Agents glow faintly in low light (trace infusion). A shard-cored artifact glows openly — because the fragment is larger and less diluted, and because the host is built to let the light out.
  • EPOCH's Mark VI+ foundry can do what ATA cannot: forge translucent structural alloys — glass-clear, blade-hard metal — so the shard's radiance shines through the whole object. The alloy is a light-pipe and a lens, never an emitter. There is nothing to switch off. Break it and it still glows; bury it and it glows in the dark under the earth.

So the sword does not glow because someone wired lights into it. It glows because there is a piece of a fallen star inside it, and the metal was made clear enough to show it. You cannot turn off a star.

The craft grammar — every mythic property is a literal feature

Assemble any artifact from these parts. Each legend-property maps to one:

The tell: brightness is a dead giveaway of how far future

The visual grade signals the origin, and every character in-era reads it instinctively even without knowing why:

  • ATA orichalcum (Mark I–V) — a coppery-red sheen, faintly luminescent in low light. Reads as precious, sacred, blessed. Subtle. A relic, a holy stone, a saint's bone that "shines."
  • EPOCH Mark VI+ (translucent, openly radiant) — impossibly clear metal lit from within. Reads as divine, otherworldly, enchanted, a gift from the gods. Unmistakable.

The more beautiful and impossible it looks, the further-future its origin — and the bigger the flag it raises. Beauty is a security risk.

The glow is a beacon — concealment is a permanent player problem

This is the design payoff of "no off-switch." A luminous shard artifact is powerful and a liability the team carries every minute they hold it, because the same shard that powers it also:

  1. Emits light that cannot be extinguished. It shines in a dark tomb, through a saddlebag, under a cloak. You cannot walk it past a night watch without planning.
  2. Radiates heat. It is always warm to the touch — a search that finds it feels it's not ordinary.
  3. Screams to agent-sense. A raw, largely-undiluted fragment registers like a bonfire. Any EPOCH operative within range feels it — and unlike a concealed agent, it cannot suppress itself. Carrying luminous anachrotech through an EPOCH-active era is carrying a lit torch through a dark house full of people who can see in the dark.

Concealment is therefore physical and imperfect, and it costs the players something every time:

  • Opaque shielding — a lead-lined case, a thick reliquary, wet clay, deep water. Dulls the light and heat; never kills the agent-sense signature entirely, and adds mass.
  • A suppression sheath (an ATA upgrade — see below) — a foundry-made scabbard/casket that damps the radiance and muffles the agent-sense hum. The best option, and something players earn.
  • Distance and timing — leave it cached; only carry it when committed; draw the glowing blade only when the fight is already lost or won and cover no longer matters.

The classic beat: the glowing sword wins the battle and blows the mission. Drawing Excalibur is a decision, not a reflex.

How luminous artifacts work as player upgrades

Anachrotech is the persistent-reward tier of the loot economy. Specifically:

  1. Yours to keep and use. A recovered artifact is simply the team's now — a powerful, character-defining piece of kit the wielder carries mission to mission. Can a PC keep an EPOCH blade? Yes. No bonding sub-system to track — whoever holds it, uses it. (For a handful of named legends the fiction gates the wielder — Excalibur "chooses" its handler, the Lady of the Lake matters — but that's a story beat for those specific artifacts, not a mechanic players manage.)
  2. Self-power = free to field. A shard-cored artifact has negative boost cost — it is fuel (Orichalcum grade table). Unlike mundane gear, hauling it across the gradient is free, even helpful. Mechanical incentive to actually carry the trophy.
  3. The glow tradeoff (above) — every use is a risk/reward decision. This keeps a "best sword in the world" from being a boring auto-win.
  4. The upgrade tree, not a one-time drop. Each artifact is an ongoing progression thread:
  • Recover it (a mission) →
  • Suppression sheath to carry it covertly (unlocks a later mission or a base-upgrade) →
  • Reverse-engineer a feature for the whole team (self-sharpening edge, a translucent-alloy sliver, the psi payload) — a real foundry-mark step toward closing EPOCH's tech lead.

One glowing sword can pay out across half a campaign.

The gear-up spine — arming for EPOCH

Luminous artifacts are the campaign's progression spine. EPOCH fields Mark VI+ everything; the players start on mundane Mark IV. The gap is the whole problem — and closing it, one legendary recovery at a time, is why many of the missions exist. Acquiring artifacts is not a side reward; it is how the team earns the right to face EPOCH on their own terms in the late campaign.

Across the arcs the team assembles a loose loadout — not a matched set, just the pieces a team needs:

SlotFlagship artifactWhat it buys
Blade (offense)ExcaliburA weapon that matches an EPOCH operative's
Shield (defense)The AegisSurvive a direct hit from Mark VI kit
Sight (psi)The NimbusPsi that matches theirs
Power (utility)The Undying FireField power, light, a charge for the rest
MobilityThe Swift SandalsKeep up with, or escape, EPOCH movement

Design guardrails so this stays play, not a checklist:

  • Each piece stands alone. It is a real upgrade the moment it's recovered — never "useless until you complete the set." There is deliberately no collect-all-five-to-unlock-a-superweapon mechanic (see the anti-trope flag in Candidates open questions).
  • Rarity paces it. Roughly one luminous artifact per arc. The team is never fully kitted until late — the campaign's rising action is the gear curve.
  • The finale is earned, not gated. Players can attempt EPOCH under-equipped; it just goes badly. The loadout is the difference between a desperate raid and a fight on their terms. That choice stays theirs.
  • EPOCH notices. A team lighting up with Mark VI recoveries becomes a priority target. The more they gear up, the more EPOCH agent-sense reads them coming — the gear that lets them win also announces them. The arc's tension scales with its own progress.

Worked examples

Excalibur — the luminous build

Extends the Excalibur candidate. An EPOCH Mark VI longsword forged of smoke-clear translucent alloy with a shard fragment set at the pommel. The fragment's radiance floods the blade end to end — it is always lit, a cold blue-white by default, warming toward gold in the hand of its keyed handler (resonance, not a switch). It is always warm, self-powered, boost-cost zero, self-sharpening. In 6th-century Britain it simply is a magic sword; no other explanation is available to anyone who sees it.

The cost is inseparable from the gift: it cannot be hidden lit, it is warm to any hand that grips it, and it is a bonfire to EPOCH agent-sense (the Lady of the Lake — almost certainly the EPOCH operative who lost it — can feel it across the valley). Owning Excalibur means everyone who matters knows you have it. Sheathing it "as ordinary" is impossible without an ATA suppression scabbard, which becomes its own recovery/build goal.

Eternal-flame gear — the ever-burning lamp

The "sacred flame that never dies" (Vesta's hearth, the perpetual lamps of countless temples, the undying forge) is a shard set in an open cradle, radiating light and heat forever with no fuel. As player kit this is a portable power cell + lantern + minor psi bed — warmth in a frozen era, light in a lightless tomb, a trickle-charge for other anachrotech, and (if the shard is large) a faint calming/aversion field a psi can shape. The catch is identical: it can't be doused, and it's a beacon. A team crossing a night border with a shard-lamp is carrying a small sun in a box.

The halo diadem — why saints glow

A head-worn shard artifact casts a ring of radiance around the wearer's head — that is the halo. Every luminous saint, bodhisattva, and god-king in religious art is, in-setting, a plausible record of someone wearing a shard-cored circlet. As kit it's a psi-amplifier crown (proximity of bulk shard to the brain accelerates psi) — and a total renunciation of anonymity. You cannot wear a halo undercover. It marks its wearer as chosen, divine, seen — which is sometimes exactly the mission.

ATA-tier, subtle: the He Shi Bi jade

Not everything blazes. An orichalcum-cored jade "luminescent in dim light" is the quiet end of the same spectrum — subtle enough that only someone unusually sensitive to shard radiance (Bian He, who lost his feet insisting it was real) can tell. Low light, low heat, low beacon. The tell scales with the tier.

Design decisions (canon)

  • Rarity — rare showpieces. Openly-luminous EPOCH artifacts are not a material class players stock up on. Each is a genuine, roughly one-per-arc event. The impossibility must stay impressive; the moment they become common loot, the wonder dies.
  • Glow-as-beacon — hard rule. The radiance, heat, and agent-sense signature are always detectable; concealment is always a cost. This is the tension the whole category exists for, not a per-artifact dial to switch off.
  • You sheath these; you don't break them. Destroying a luminous artifact to kill its glow is not routine tradecraft — it is near-indestructible star-forged future alloy, and smashing a priceless blade to hide a torch is absurd. The suppression scabbard/casket is the canonical solution to concealment. Shard extraction or destruction happens only for story reasons — a sacrifice, a ritual, denying it to EPOCH at the wire — never as a routine plan.

Open questions

  • Suppression sheaths. Are these ATA-standard issue for any recovered artifact, or a per-artifact custom build (each one a mission/upgrade)? The latter is more play, and fits the "rare showpiece" framing — each legendary blade earns its own legendary scabbard.
  • Overload / cracked cores. A damaged shard cradle over-radiates — a luminous artifact that's been struck hard becomes brighter, hotter, and eventually dangerous (raw exposure over-saturates and kills). A "the sword is glowing too bright" panic beat.
