# The Temporal Gradient

How agents move through time and space.

## The Premise

Time travel is not "step into a machine, arrive in 44 BCE." It is **gradient navigation** — riding currents of meteor-radiated parachronic energy that extend outward from each shard's impact moment, with cost scaling by **4D spacetime distance** from the shard anchor.

## The Core Model

Each shard creates an independent parachronic gradient. Think of it as a **gravity well in spacetime** — the shard sits at the bottom in both space and time, and the surrounding spacetime rises up the slope away from it in every direction.

The gradient is **bidirectional in time** (extending both forward and backward from the shard's moment of impact) and **omnidirectional in space** (extending outward from the shard's geographic location). The maximum reach is finite, proportional to the shard's mass, and the same shape in every direction — a 4D spheroid centered on the anchor.

- **Cost scales with 4D distance from anchor.** Temporal separation dominates the cost (a millennium counts for vastly more than circumnavigating the globe), but **spatial separation contributes meaningfully**. Reaching Bronze Age China from the Origin costs more than reaching Bronze Age Egypt — same era, different spatial vector.
- **Climbing the gradient is hard, falling down it is cheap.** Travel cost is *asymmetric* depending on direction relative to the anchor. See [Boost vs. Drop Transit](#boost-vs-drop-transit) below.
- **Each shard has a maximum reach** — beyond that 4D distance, the gradient is too shallow to navigate even at maximum boost (effectively infinite cost).
- **Travelers ride from wherever they currently are** to wherever they want to go. Their starting position is anywhere along the gradient.
- **Embedded agents reduce effective spatial cost.** A long-term embed in a distant region serves as a *secondary anchor* — missions inserting near them pay a reduced spatial component, because the embed's infused tissue provides a local landing point. This is the major operational reason embeds are infrastructure, not just intelligence.

### Why ATA and EPOCH Can't Strike Each Other Decisively

A practical consequence of the 4D distance model: the two agencies' home bases are far apart in *both* dimensions. Kunlun is in Tibet (~6000 km from the Origin) and impacted in ~2200 CE (~6000 years after the Origin). Either side trying to reach the other's headquarters pays the full 4D cost — a boost transit larger than any operational mission either side runs in normal play. This is the structural reason the rivalry is fought as shadow operations in shared mid-zone eras rather than direct attacks on the rival's anchor. MAD doctrine reinforces it. The physics enforces it.

## Boost vs. Drop Transit

Time travel is **gravity-well navigation**. Each shard sits at the bottom of a temporal gravity well. Eras far from the anchor are "uphill." Returning to the anchor is downhill.

### Boost Transit (anchor → distant era)

Climbing away from the shard. **Expensive.**

- Burns the lion's share of the fuel charge.
- Carrying capacity is *strictly limited* — every kilogram of equipment you bring increases the climb cost. Operators travel light: era-appropriate clothing, one weapon if any, minimal modern gear, period-currency seeded by embed agents in advance.
- **Orichalcum-infused gear costs dramatically less** to boost than mundane matter — this is the entire purpose of the agency foundry. See [Orichalcum.md](Orichalcum.md). Standard-issue ATA equipment is infused at ~1% by mass for roughly 40% boost-cost reduction. Modern equipment that *isn't* infused (improvised acquisitions, captured EPOCH tech still being analyzed) is rationed by Outfitting before insertion — they will tell you no.
- Heavily-laden boost transits sometimes *fail* mid-climb, dumping the team into an era they didn't choose (closer to the anchor than intended). "Short jumps" of this kind are a real operational hazard.

### Drop Transit (distant era → anchor)

Falling back down the gradient. **Cheap.**

- Burns a small fraction of the fuel a boost of equivalent distance would cost.
- Carrying capacity is *generous* — loot, artifacts, captured EPOCH gear, biological samples, even live captures can come down the well with minimal cost penalty.
- This is **the extraction profile** every team plans around.
- Triggered by each agent's personal [Antikythera device](Antikythera-Device.md), which contains the return-coordinate programming and is the agent-side initiator of the drop. Without a functioning device, an agent cannot self-initiate drop transit; they must travel as a passenger on a teammate's drop, or be retrieved by a follow-up extraction mission.

### Lateral Transit (era → another era, neither at anchor)

Moving across the gradient without returning to anchor first. **Possible but rare.**

- Costs scale with the *altitude change* between origin era and destination era, not the raw temporal distance between them.
- A lateral jump from 500 BCE to 1500 CE costs about the same as a fresh boost from anchor to 1500 CE — because you're climbing back up the well from a lower point.
- Most teams instead drop home, refit, and boost out again. Lateral transit is for emergencies, deep-cover operations, or situations where surfacing to anchor would blow the mission.

### The Gameplay Loop

This asymmetry creates the standard RPG loop:

1. **Brief at home base** — Atlantis (ATA) or Shambhala (EPOCH).
2. **Boost out light.** Climb the well to the target era with minimum gear, maximum personnel.
3. **Operate on local economy.** Embedded agents, period-appropriate supplies, in-era contacts. Modern tech is a luxury, not a default.
4. **Drop home heavy.** Bring back intel, artifacts, captured assets, salvaged biotech, period valuables. Everything you took *plus* everything you found.
5. **Trade and research.** Recovered material funds the agency and accelerates ATA's tech base (especially anything recovered from EPOCH operations).

### Implications

- **Embedded agents are infrastructure.** Long-embed operatives can't be resupplied easily — every care package costs full boost. They maintain their own period-appropriate economic base or do without.
- **Loot economy is real.** ATA's modern-era operations are partially funded by Bronze Age gold, Renaissance art (acquired before its provenance becomes traceable), and historical artifacts that can be quietly placed in collections.
- **EPOCH faces the same physics.** Their boost out of 2200 CE is just as expensive as ATA's boost out of 2026 CE. But their drops home from the players' present are cheap — which is why captured EPOCH agents are so hard to *prevent extracting*. Cut them loose for one moment and they fall up the well.
- **The "stranded agent" trope is real.** An operative whose embed network collapses may not have the fuel reserve for a boost extraction, but a passing teammate can almost always drop them home. This shapes mission planning and rescue doctrine.

## Personal Transit Tolerance — the Far Traveler Advantage

The fuel and carry costs above are the *agency's* cost. There is also a
**per-agent cost**: transiting far from your native point on the gradient
physically taxes the body. The greater the 4D distance you personally span, the
worse the **transit strain** — disorientation, cumulative health penalties, and
longer recovery before you can operate.

This is bought down with character points. A GURPS-style advantage —
**Far Traveler** (levels of gradient tolerance) — reduces the strain and extends
the range of eras and regions an agent can reach and still function in. It is a
real point sink, and that makes it a self-balancing build axis:

- An agent built to reach an *extreme* corner of the gradient spends heavily on
  Far Traveler, leaving fewer points for combat, skills, and social capability.
  **Far-reachers are specialists, not generalists** — formidable at arriving
  where almost no one else can, comparatively soft once they're there.
- An operative who only works near their native era pays nothing for reach and
  pours everything into being *dangerous*. This is why the most lethal opponents
  a team faces tend to be **home-turf, near-era** agents — not exotic
  far-travelers.

Worked examples:

- **Helen (EPOCH)** in the Bronze-Age Mediterranean is doubly distant from Kunlun
  (deep past *and* far west). Reaching it at all marks her as a rare, heavily
  Far-Traveler-invested build — which is exactly why she leans on seduction,
  infiltration, and leverage rather than force. Her *reach* is the threat;
  toe-to-toe she is beatable.
- **Chief (PC)**, native to a cyberpunk far-future, pays the same tax in reverse:
  operating in the present-day campaign zone is *his* long climb, so a chunk of
  his sheet is spent simply being here. Same constraint, opposite direction.

Design effect: the gradient's cost is felt at the character level, not just on
the fuel ledger, and far-flung antagonists balance themselves — their reach is
paid for in everything else.

## Implications

- **Each agency has a "home turf" *region-era*.** Spacetime near the shard anchor is cheap to reach and cheap to return from. Spacetime far from the anchor — in either dimension — is expensive to boost into, but still cheap to drop *back* from.
- **Geography costs.** You can travel to any geographic location, but distant regions add to the 4D cost. East Asia is materially more expensive for ATA than the Mediterranean for the same era; Western Europe is correspondingly more expensive for EPOCH.
- **Anchoring via embeds.** Agents need a physical or biological anchor at their destination to land precisely *and* to reduce the spatial-cost component. This is why so many agents *live* in their target eras, embedded as historical figures — they ARE the anchor for follow-up ops, and every embed established in a distant region is a permanent capital improvement to that region's accessibility.
- **Asymmetric two-way travel.** Returning home is dramatically cheaper than getting out there in the first place (the well is downhill on the way back, in all four dimensions). See [Boost vs. Drop Transit](#boost-vs-drop-transit). An agent embedded in 1500 BCE Egypt *can* drop home cheaply at any time — what strands embeds isn't return cost, it's the loss of an extraction anchor or political conditions that prevent surfacing to one.
- **Anchor network density is a strategic asset.** See [Anchor Network Density](#anchor-network-density) below.

## Anchor Network Density

Where each agency has dense embed coverage, missions to that region run cheaper. Where coverage is sparse, every fresh mission pays the full spatial-cost component to insert. This means the *geographic distribution* of agency embeds is a real long-arc campaign asset — building anchors in new regions is a strategic capital improvement, and disrupting the rival's anchor network in a region is a long-game offensive move.

### ATA's Anchor Network (typical density)

| Region | Density | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mediterranean / Levant / Egypt | Dense | Adjacent to the Origin spatially; densely embedded across eras |
| Western Europe | Dense | Cultural+political proximity to ATA modern leadership |
| Eastern Europe / Russia | Moderate | Variable across eras; sparse in deep antiquity, denser in modern |
| North Africa | Moderate | Adjacent spatial, but anchor-establishment was historically uneven |
| East Asia (China, Japan, Korea) | **Sparse** | Spatially distant; each embed required a major insertion investment |
| India / South Asia | Sparse | Spatially distant; historically minor ATA priority |
| Sub-Saharan Africa | Sparse | Spatially distant; historically minor ATA priority |
| Americas (pre-1500) | Very sparse | Both spatially and operationally remote; only a handful of anchors exist |

### EPOCH's Anchor Network (typical density)

| Region | Density | Why |
|---|---|---|
| East Asia (China, Japan, Korea, Mongolia) | Dense | Adjacent to Kunlun spatially; EPOCH's regional home |
| Central Asia / Tibet / Himalayas | Dense | Immediate Kunlun vicinity |
| Russia / Eastern Europe | Moderate | Spatially closer than the Mediterranean; cultural-political extension of the 2200s hegemony's reach |
| Western Europe | **Sparse** | Spatially distant; every embed required a major insertion investment from Kunlun |
| Mediterranean / Levant | Sparse | Spatially distant; contested with ATA at high cost |
| Americas | Sparse | Operationally remote in both directions |

### What This Means for Mission Design

- **East Asian campaigns are *contested with EPOCH home-field advantage*.** ATA teams insert as the away team. They pay more spatial cost, they have fewer local embeds, the locals trust the EPOCH-aligned figures more. This is structurally a different mission shape than a Mediterranean op.
- **Mediterranean campaigns are the mirror image.** ATA home-field; EPOCH inserts as the away team.
- **India, sub-Saharan Africa, and the Americas are *spatially expensive for both sides*.** A 4D-distant target like Tang-era Vietnam or Maurya-era India is a real reach for either agency. These regions are natural settings for third-party operations (see [../GM-Secrets/Other-Shards.md](../GM-Secrets/Other-Shards.md)) and for *anchor-establishment* campaigns where one side invests heavily to claim a new sphere of influence.
- **Long-arc strategic play:** ATA agreeing to spend a high-cost mission establishing one embed in Tang Dynasty China makes every *subsequent* Tang Chinese mission run cheaper. This is the kind of decision agency leadership debates for months in fiction, and that players can engage with as long-game capital improvement.

## Materiel

- Meteor shards are the only known temporal fuel source.
- ATA refines and dispenses temporal energy from the Origin in measured doses — call them **charges**.
- EPOCH operates from the [Kunlun Shard](The-Kunlun-Shard.md) and refines its own charges. Their reserve is smaller per unit time because their shard is smaller.

## Mutability

The timeline is **single, mutable, and propagates consequences forward**. ## The Two Known Gradients

### The Origin (Mediterranean, ATA's gradient)

- **Anchor:** ~4000 BCE.
- **Max reach:** ~6000 years in either direction.
- **Operational range:** ~4000 BCE → ~2050 CE.
- **Backward extension:** the gradient extends into pre-Origin prehistory but reaches no civilization, so the extension is operationally useless.
- **Forward limit:** ATA can barely reach a couple decades past the present. Future operations are not practical.

### The Kunlun Shard (Tibet, EPOCH's gradient — *located in the future*)

- **Anchor:** ~2200 CE.
- **Max reach:** ~4500 years in either direction (smaller shard, shorter reach).
- **Operational range:** ~2300 BCE → ~6700 CE.
- **Backward limit:** EPOCH **cannot reach the deep Bronze Age (4000–2300 BCE)** at all. This is ATA's exclusive domain.
- **Forward extension:** EPOCH can reach centuries past their anchor into deeper future — outside the campaign frame.

See [The-Kunlun-Shard.md](The-Kunlun-Shard.md) for the full implications of EPOCH being a *future* agency rather than a modern one.

### The Resulting Zones (Temporal Only)

This table tracks *temporal* reach. See [Anchor Network Density](#anchor-network-density) above for how spatial cost overlays on top of this.

| Era | ATA access | EPOCH access | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4000–2300 BCE | Yes (cheap-moderate) | **No** | ATA exclusive (temporally) |
| 2300 BCE – 1 CE | Yes (cheap-moderate) | Yes (expensive) | Contested, ATA-favored |
| 1 CE – 1500 CE | Yes (moderate) | Yes (moderate) | Heavily contested middle |
| 1500–2026 CE | Yes (moderate-expensive) | Yes (cheap-moderate) | Contested, EPOCH-favored |
| 2026–2050 CE | Yes (expensive, edge) | Yes (cheap) | EPOCH-dominant near future |
| 2050+ CE | **No** | Yes | EPOCH exclusive; outside campaign |

The **playable contested zone** — where the campaign actually happens — is roughly 2300 BCE to 2050 CE. Everything before is ATA solo missions; everything after is unreachable to the players (and to ATA itself).

Within the playable zone, the *geographic* dimension creates further structure: even in a temporally-contested era, East Asian missions are EPOCH-favored and Mediterranean missions are ATA-favored, with India, Africa, and the Americas as harder-for-both reach zones.
