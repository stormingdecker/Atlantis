# Aliases and Identity

How agents protect themselves across the timeline.

## The Core Rule

**Every agent operates under an alias.** Real names are agency-classified. Field operations, mission briefings, social interactions, and even most internal communications use codenames rather than birth names. The convention is enforced doctrinally on both sides — ATA and EPOCH both maintain it for the same operational reason.

## Why

The agency war is fought across thousands of years. An EPOCH counter-intelligence operation that learns an ATA agent's *real* identity gains access to a powerful threat vector: **retroactive elimination**. If the rival knows when and where an agent was born, who their parents were, what village they came from — they can dispatch a backward-projected operative to that earlier moment and *prevent the agent from ever being recruited*. The agent vanishes from the timeline. Their service to the rival ceases to have happened.

This is not theoretical. It has happened. ATA has lost agents this way; EPOCH has lost agents this way. Modern senior leadership on both sides has personal experience with timelines in which colleagues simply *stopped existing* — with no memory among the rank-and-file that they ever had.

The alias convention is the agency's primary defense against this threat.

## How the Convention Works

- **Aliases are operational identities.** A field agent named "Helen" has an entire constructed history — birthplace, family, schooling, recruitment path — that traces back to a *fictional* person. Investigations into "Helen's" past hit a dead end at the fabricated layer.
- **Real identities are siloed.** Only the agent's handler, a small group of senior leadership, and the recruitment officer know an agent's birth identity. Even within the agency, this information is need-to-know.
- **Records of recruitment are destroyed.** Once an agent completes infusion and field training, agency archives often *physically destroy* the pre-recruitment identity records. The information persists in the heads of a few people; it is not preserved in any searchable form.
- **Some agents deliberately forget.** Under hypnotic conditioning during recruitment, some agents have their own pre-recruitment memories suppressed. They cannot tell EPOCH their real identity because they no longer know it. This is voluntary and reversible (the memories can be restored), but for active-service operatives it is the most secure form of identity protection.
- **Aliases are sometimes inherited.** Senior operative roles occasionally pass their alias to a successor. The "Chief" who runs current operations may or may not be the *same person* who was Chief in 1920. The continuity of the codename masks the discontinuity of the wearer. (Whether this is true of the current Chief specifically is GM-knowledge — see [../Embedded-Agents/Chief.md](../Embedded-Agents/Chief.md).)
- **Period-appropriate cover identities are layered on top of aliases.** Helen-the-EPOCH-operative living in Sparta as "Helen of Sparta" was wearing *two* identities — her agent alias (Helen) and her in-period cover (Helen of Sparta, royal, daughter of Tyndareus). The historical record remembers the cover; agency records use the alias.

## Consequences for the Campaign

### 1. Player characters use aliases

Whatever the player calls their PC is the PC's *operational name*. The character's birth identity is either unknown to the player, classified, or actively forgotten. Players should pick alias names with this in mind — codenames, period-appropriate cover identities, or other constructed names. "Real-sounding" names work fine as long as the player understands the in-fiction status.

If a player wants their PC to *know* their own real name and birth history, that's a deliberate roleplay choice with mechanical consequences: the PC is more vulnerable to retroactive elimination, and the GM should treat that knowledge as a campaign-long liability.

### 2. NPCs use aliases

Chief is an alias. Helen is an alias. Hatshepsut is the historical-record cover identity adopted by an ATA agent whose real identity is in agency archives somewhere (and possibly nowhere — see "deliberately forgotten" above). Every named agent in the campaign is operating under cover.

This is why the [Embedded-Agents/Candidates.md](../Embedded-Agents/Candidates.md) roster reads as full of historical figures whose pre-agency lives are *opaque* — that opacity is *deliberate*, agency-engineered, and reflects the alias convention as it operates across history.

### 3. Real identity is a high-value intelligence asset

A captured ATA agent's *alias* is worth modest intel; their *real name* is worth significantly more. EPOCH interrogators prioritize real-name extraction. Sophisticated agents under interrogation will offer plausible *false* real names to satisfy the interrogator while protecting the truth — a final layer of cover.

### 4. Counter-intelligence missions can target *real* identities

Finding out who an EPOCH agent actually was/will be — their pre-recruitment birth identity — opens a brutal mission type: **retroactive elimination**. Dispatch a team to that person's pre-recruitment era, intercept them before the agency ever found them, and remove them from the timeline. The target's service to the rival agency ceases to have happened.

- This is *war crime* material by any modern ethical standard.
- ATA's official doctrine forbids it. ATA in *practice* has done it under exceptional circumstances and may again.
- EPOCH does it more readily. Several ATA operations have been *retroactively erased* by EPOCH retro-elimination in agency memory; the senior leadership remembers, the Reality Log has the gap, but the operations themselves never happened.
- Whether the players ever face this choice is a major campaign moral hinge. The campaign should leave room for it as an option without forcing it.

### 5. Some real identities are lost forever

Long-service agents whose recruitment records were destroyed centuries ago, who themselves no longer remember, have *unrecoverable* real identities. They are safe from retroactive elimination but also, in a deep way, *no longer who they used to be*. This is one of the campaign's quiet melancholies. The senior cohort at modern Atlantis includes several agents in this category.

### 6. Some aliases are visible enough that the alias itself is targeted

When EPOCH cannot identify a specific ATA agent's real name, they sometimes pursue *the alias's known operations* instead — building a profile of "Helen" or "Caesar" or whoever, mapping their operational era and patterns, and predicting where the agent (under that name or any name) is likely to appear next. This is *operational* counter-intelligence as distinct from *identity* counter-intelligence. It is less devastating than retroactive elimination but more common.

## Alias Convention Practice

- **Most aliases are unrelated to the agent's real identity.** Helen, Hatshepsut, Chief — none of these names contain identifying information about the person wearing them.
- **Codename collisions happen.** Multiple agents may have used "Caesar" or "Apollo" or other common codenames across centuries. Disambiguation by era, by handler, or by additional designators.
- **Aliases can be retired.** When an alias becomes too widely known, agents sometimes retire their codename and adopt a new one. Helen's modern-era retirement (or possibly her death) would mean the *next* operative to need a glamour-augmented Mediterranean operational identity would receive a fresh codename.
- **Cross-faction respect.** EPOCH agents observe the same convention. When ATA captures an EPOCH operative, the captive identifies themselves by their *alias*, not their birth name. Mutual interrogation games revolve around real-name extraction.

## Open Questions

- Are there agents whose real names have been *deliberately destroyed* in agency records and *also forgotten by themselves*? If so, they are now operationally unkillable retroactively — and also untethered from human history. Worth flagging as a roleplay angle.
- How do agents handle relationships with non-agents who knew them pre-recruitment? Family members, old friends. Cut contact entirely? Maintain limited contact under cover? The doctrine is "cut contact"; the practice is varied.
- What happens if a player character's real name is *discovered by EPOCH mid-campaign*? Does the campaign play out the retroactive elimination, with the player's character vanishing? Is there a counter-mission to prevent it? Worth pre-thinking as a possible session structure.
- **Whose real name does Chief know?** As a senior leader, Chief presumably knows the real identities of a small number of operatives. Players sometimes try to find out whose names he holds. He never says.
- The historical record's blank spots — where a famous figure's early life is unusually opaque, the alias convention is a partial explanation. (The early lives of Helen, Hatshepsut, Cleopatra, Theodora, Joan of Arc, etc. all share this property in the real historical record. Coincidence, or agency engineering?)
